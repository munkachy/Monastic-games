FEAR NOT

A desert monk, his guardian angel, and a city of shadows
Design page · a browser game for phones · the first build is playable

The idea

Fr. Lawrence, an ordinary priest-monk, lives in a small monastery in the desert. In the middle of the night his guardian angel wakes him: "We have work to do." His body stays kneeling in the chapel while he is carried, by bilocation, to a city far away. There he and the angel fly over the rooftops, find where souls are under attack, and drop down to fight the demons tempting them. The person, who sees nothing, makes their own choice. Win back a district and its lights come on again.

The aim is to make the priesthood look as cool and as interesting as Batman: a quiet man by day, and by night something no one in the city knows about.

The priest

  • Thoroughly average. He forgets things, he is a bit clumsy, and he never had any great talent. That is why he thought he was meant for a contemplative life. He accepted it and was peaceful and happy in the desert.
  • He protests. "Wait a minute. I'm not Padre Pio!" He stands in a long line: Moses ("I am slow of speech", Exodus 4:10), Jeremiah ("I am a child", Jeremiah 1:6), Gideon, the least in his father's house (Judges 6:15). There are saints like him too: St. John Vianney nearly failed the seminary, and St. Joseph of Cupertino was thought dull.
  • The angel's answer. God knows all his weaknesses and means to show His power through them: "power is made perfect in infirmity" (2 Corinthians 12:9, Douay-Rheims).
  • What he can do that no angel can. The angels are far mightier, but only the priest can absolve, anoint the dying and bless with a priest's blessing. Every big encounter ends with what only he can give. A confession is never shown or overheard: the view turns away (the seal).
  • His weakness is part of the game. When he stumbles, the angel catches him and the stumble becomes a move. When he drops the holy water, it splashes the demon behind him. When he forgets the words of a prayer, the angel prompts him. His mistakes turn into grace.
  • He grew up on kung fu films. As a boy he watched Hong Kong kung fu movies over and over. He gave them up when he entered a monastery with no television. Now, when the angel shows him the spiritual battle, he sees it as kung fu, because those are the pictures his mind still holds. St. Thomas teaches that an angel can stir a person's imagination, but only with images already there, as a man born blind cannot be shown colour (ST I q.111 a.3). So the whole look of the fighting is explained by the story. Nothing given up for God is lost (Mark 10:29–30); He gives it back, changed.
  • Drunken master. His fighting style is drunken-fist kung fu: he sways, staggers, nearly falls, and every stumble turns into a strike or a dodge. But he is not drunk on wine. At Pentecost the crowd mocked the apostles, "These men are full of new wine" (Acts 2:13), and St. Paul says "be not drunk with wine... but be ye filled with the holy Spirit" (Ephesians 5:18). St. Ambrose's hymn at Lauds sings of the "sober drunkenness of the Spirit" (sobriam ebrietatem Spiritus, in Splendor paternae gloriae). His style is that: Sobria Ebrietas.
  • The Spirit meter. Some drunken fighters in games grow wilder and stronger the more they drink. Fr. Lawrence's meter fills with prayer and with a flowing combo instead. Each level loosens his sway and opens new moves.
  • A running joke. The angel: "If you had grown up on westerns, this would be a showdown at noon."
  • The night has an end. He must be back before the bell for Vigils. Each night is one session; at the bell he wakes in the chapel, and the brothers never know.

The desert and the city

  • The desert is warm and quiet: adobe walls, red cliffs, stars, the chapel candle. It is home and the place he returns to.
  • The city is cold film noir: rain, black and grey, gold only where there is light.
  • The contrast between the two is the look of the whole game. The monastery is made up, in the spirit of the desert monasteries of New Mexico, not a real house.

The name comes from the first words angels speak, again and again, in Scripture. They are said to the person on the bench, and to the player.

Look and feel

  • Neon noir, today. A present-day city at night, the colours of the John Wick films: deep navy and near-black, rain, wet streets that mirror the light, and neon everywhere: signs in red, hot pink, cyan and teal, with a little gold. Every light source glows and stands out: neon, streetlamps, lit windows, headlights, candles, church windows.
  • The palette.
    Gold is kept for holy light: the angel, candles, church windows.
  • The art. Simple, elegant, flat shapes: silhouettes in long coats and hats, clean edges, strong rim light from the neon, rain drawn in straight streaks. Faces are simple, or left in shadow.
  • Faces. Angels' faces are a soft blank glare of light; demons' faces are blank darkness with a faint ember glow. Clean, eerie and anime-like.
  • Cut scenes in close-up. Many shots, not all, push in tight: an eye filling the screen, a mouth, hands on a rosary, rain on a hat brim. Flat colour, a few sharp shapes, neon light down one side of the face. The style is like Sergio Leone's westerns, which cut to the eyes before a showdown; that fits the angel's joke about westerns. It also echoes icons, where the eyes are large and everything is in the gaze. Close-ups are also cheap to draw well: one strong picture instead of a whole scene.
  • Cut scene shots. They move between tight close-ups and medium side views of a figure standing in front of the skyline, still framed close. The dialogue sits in a clean band below the picture. Everything slick and classy: few words, plain type, slow cuts.
  • Whose eyes. Fr. Lawrence's eyes are tired and kind. The angel's face is too bright to look at: the close-up shows only light, and perhaps the faint shape of an eye within it. A demon's close-up is darkness with an ember where the eye should be. The people's eyes show what they are going through.
  • Long, slender figures. Drawn from jointed parts moved in code, like SaintStyle Turbo's saints, but everyone a little long and slender, with arms and legs slightly longer than life. In a fight the limbs fly wide, so the kung fu reads beautifully from across the screen. Think of El Greco, whose saints are drawn out long as if pulled toward heaven.
  • Tone. The demons take all the slapstick; the angels keep their dignity. Defeated demons are cast out, vanishing with a yelp in a puff of light, never killed (spirits cannot be destroyed).
  • Music. Noir jazz while flying and walking the city. When a fight starts it turns to kung fu film music, and when the fight ends it slides back into the jazz (the synth engine from Luminaries).

Flying the city

An open world from above, in 2D. The city is seen from straight above, with Fr. Lawrence riding his angel over it. It looks 3D through three cheap tricks:

  • Leaning buildings. Each building is drawn as its roof plus its walls, and the taller it is, the further its roof leans away from the centre of the screen. As you fly past, the towers seem to swing, and you see the neon signs on their walls. This is how the first top-down Grand Theft Auto games made a flat city feel deep.
  • Height is zoom. Climb and the camera pulls back, so the city shrinks below you; dive and it rushes up, the rooftops growing larger. Clouds and rain drift between you and the ground at their own speed.
  • A shadow on the roofs. The angel's shadow falls on the rooftops below, small and far away when you are high, close beneath you when you dive. That shows your height at a glance.

The joins come naturally: dive to the street and the view cuts, often through a close-up, to the side-on fight; win, and the angel lifts off and the camera rises back to the view from above. The skyline lives in the cut scenes and the side-on fights.

  • Above the fog. Low down, the fog and the dark drain you and hide the way. Climb high enough and you break out above the fog into clear night air, with the neon glowing up through it.
  • Churches recharge you. Fly near a church and its light fills you again; a lit church is a place to rest and recover.

Light is your anchor; shadow is the demons' ground. This is what web-slinging is to Spider-Man.

Fighting

Flowing crowd combat, as in the Batman Arkham games, played with touch alone: no D-pad, no clutter. Many demons at once; you flow from one to the next.

  • Dizzy stars. A demon beaten enough is dazed for a moment: an opening.
  • Rage. Sometimes a demon rages and attacking it is useless; dodge until it calms. (Both from Batman: Arkham City Lockdown on phones.)
  • Combo buttons. At, say, ×5, ×10 and ×20 hits, special buttons light up in the corners; a hit taken resets the count.

The Heights and the Depths

Like the uppercut in Mortal Kombat that sends a fighter through the ceiling to another level.

  • Rise to the Heights. Uppercut a dizzy demon and you both burst up into the clouds. Up there angels are stronger: wider counter windows, faster combos, light restores you.
  • Dragged to the Depths. Some demons grab; miss the dodge and you are both dragged into a dark under-layer. Demons hit harder there, your combo fades faster, whispers distract you. Fight your way back up by reaching a combo count, for a big reward.
  • These are pictures of the spiritual struggle, not literal places.

The priest, the angels, and tag-team

You play the priest. At first only his guardian angel fights beside him and joins his combos. As more of the city is won back, the archangels come and show up in his combos too. The priest's own moves are blessings, holy water, the stole, the Name of Jesus and the sign of the cross, done with flair. The angels strike the heavy blows.

At a high combo a tag button appears and an angel swoops in for a move. The long goal: gather the archangels, and grow the priest in virtue.

"What you see is combat." Early on, the angel tells him: "What you will see is a fight. What is really happening is beyond what you can understand. I am showing it to you with what you already have." The fights are a picture of a spiritual struggle, drawn from the kung fu films of his boyhood, and the game says so.

The look of the fights follows those films: wire-work leaps, long poses, whip-fast exchanges, sound effects on every blow, and the long limbs of the figures flying wide.

  • Never a plain walk. When he goes from one demon to the next, he always flips, rolls, or staggers across in a drunken lurch that ends in the strike.
  • The angel blocks. Sometimes, when he dodges, his angel steps in and takes the blow for him.
  • The angel strikes. In some combos the angel comes in for a hit while Fr. Lawrence steps aside, then they trade places again.
  • Ideas to study. Drunken fighters in other games: Lei Wulong in Tekken, Shun Di in Virtua Fighter, Brad Wong in Dead or Alive, Jamie in Street Fighter 6 (his moves grow with his "drink level"), and the Stagger style in Absolver, which dodges by swaying. All take their look from Hong Kong drunken-fist films.

The story

  • The opening is the tutorial: the night the angel wakes him, his protest, the first flight and the first fight. See "The first build" below.
  • Not princes. His angel is a guardian, from the lowest choir. As in Daniel 10, the way into some parts of the city is held against them, and they must fight to get through.
  • The princes. The darkness over each part of the city is ruled by a fallen prince. Breaking a prince's hold is what brings an archangel to fight beside them: Michael first, as he came to help in Daniel 10:13.
  • He rides on the angel's back. A priest cannot fly. He hangs on to his angel's back, cassock flapping, holding his hat on; the light-tether is the angel's reach.
  • The holy souls. Some places are dark because a soul in purgatory is bound up with them and asks for help. The angel cannot set that soul free alone. What he can do is move a living person to pray for it, or to have a Mass offered. When that prayer is made, that part of the city opens. St. Gregory the Great tells stories of this kind in Book IV of his Dialogues, the same work whose second book is the life of St. Benedict.

The people

A handful of people you meet again and again, whose lives change over the game:

  • a young priest
  • a widow
  • a child
  • someone fighting an addiction
  • a businesswoman uptown
  • a man who lives with same-sex attraction. His story shows what the Church actually teaches: no one is defined by their sexuality. A person is first God's creature and, by grace, God's child (CDF, Letter on the Pastoral Care of Homosexual Persons, 1986, no. 16). The demons' attack on him is mostly loneliness and contempt, and the answer is real friendship, the kind St. Aelred of Rievaulx wrote about in Spiritual Friendship.

Encounters

The overpowered guardian. In every fight, the person's own guardian angel is there, held down by the demons: pinned in chains of shadow, calling for help. That call is what Fr. Lawrence's angel hears. Break the chains during the fight and the guardian rises to fight beside you for a moment.

Afterwards the freed guardian can tell what he has seen, and that moves the story on: a name, a place, where the darkness began. A guardian knows the person's life and what has been done to them, but not the secrets of their heart, which God alone sees (ST I q.57 a.4).

The guardian is held back, never wounded or destroyed, and never fails in love: like the angel in Daniel 10, withstood until help came. What weakens his hold is mostly the person's own choices, which let the darkness in.

The loop: sense → fly → swoop down → the view changes to the side-on fight → the person chooses → back to the sky.

Fighting never decides for the person. It clears the fog around them and strengthens their resolve; at the end they choose. Sometimes they still fall, and the story brings you back later (perhaps they are on their way to confession, and the demons are blocking the door).

The heavy ones are handled with care: real hope, the person's own choice, grace, the sacraments, a friend who shows up; never a person shown as simply "defeated".

The city

  • Near churches: gentler. More updrafts, easier fights, holy hours as safe places.
  • The poor quarter: many encounters, but also many prayers rising and many updrafts. The poor are often rich in faith; it is never "poor means sinful".
  • Uptown: looks calm, is among the hardest: respectable-looking demons of pride, greed and quiet despair.
  • Every district starts in shadow and lightens as you win it back.
  • How it is built. The districts are placed by hand: where each church, landmark and boss is. The buildings in between are generated from a fixed seed, so the same city comes back every time and only a little has to be saved (which districts are lit). They are simple tall shapes.
  • A floor in the air. In flight you cannot drop below the rooftops; the streets are lost in fog. You only go down to the street for a fight, which keeps the city cheap to draw on a phone.

Losing, and difficulty

  • When the priest is beaten, his angel carries him back to the nearest church to recover. No death.
  • When a person falls, they grow shadowy. It is never final: while they live they can always repent, and the story brings you back to them.
  • Normal and Hard. On Hard the demons are tougher and quicker.

Growing stronger

  • The priest grows; the angels do not change rank. In St. Thomas's account an angel's place is fixed by its nature. The priest is the one who grows.
  • The four virtues: Fortitude (fighting), Prudence (wider counter window, sharper sensing), Temperance (breath and stamina), Justice (protecting others).
  • New commissions: new districts and missions entrusted as you prove yourself.
  • Joy: victories add to an angel's "accidental" glory, which St. Thomas says can grow until the Last Day (ST I q.62 a.9 ad 3).

Theology, kept straight

  • Names: only Michael, Gabriel and Raphael are named; others go by title (Directory on Popular Piety and the Liturgy, no. 217).
  • Angels can enlighten the mind and act on the imagination, but cannot move the will (ST I q.111). Demons work by the same means.
  • The rank of archangel sits just above the angels (Pseudo-Dionysius), and guardians come ordinarily from the lowest rank (ST I q.113 a.3), so the guardian angel and the archangels fit together naturally.
  • Battles can be long and resisted: the prince of Persia withstood an angel for twenty-one days until Michael came (Daniel 10:13).
  • Bilocation is told of many saints: St. Alphonsus Liguori, St. Martin de Porres, Ven. Mary of Ágreda, St. Pio. How it happens is a matter of theological opinion. Many theologians hold that a body cannot be fully present in two places at once, and explain that the person is truly in one place and made present in the other by God, through an angel. That fits the game well: the angel is how he is there.
  • Exorcism. A solemn exorcism needs the bishop's express permission (Code of Canon Law, can. 1172). In the city the priest prays, blesses and resists temptation; he does not perform solemn exorcisms.
  • The souls of the dead cannot possess anyone, and a human soul never becomes a demon. At death each soul is judged at once (Benedict XII, Benedictus Deus, 1336). By God's leave a soul in purgatory may appear to the living to ask for prayer (St. Thomas, ST Suppl. q.69 a.3). Some exorcists have claimed otherwise about possession, but that is private opinion, and the game does not follow it.
  • On suicide, the Church does not despair of anyone's salvation: "By ways known to him alone, God can provide the opportunity for salutary repentance" (Catechism, no. 2283).
  • The lines the angels and saints speak are written for the game, in their spirit, not presented as quotations.

The first build

A small but complete piece of the game, played sideways on a phone:

  1. Cold open: "Bring Daddy Home". 2 AM. A radio in the dark: for a week the lights have been going out across the city, block by block. In a small apartment a girl of about seven kneels at her window in her pyjamas: "Please bring Daddy home." Out in the rain her father sits in his car across from a corner store, deep in debt to the wrong people, a gun in his lap. Shapes in the dark lean close and whisper: Just this once. Nobody gets hurt. You have no choice.
  2. The desert. Hard cut to silence and stars. Fr. Lawrence asleep in his cell. A light. "Get up. Someone asked." The one who asked is a child. His protest ("I'm not Padre Pio"), and the angel's answer.
  3. The flight. Riding the angel's back over the district from above: learning to steer, climb above the fog, tether to lights, and recharge near a church, while the darkness closes round the car.
  4. The fight. Down to the wet street around the car, side-on, against the demons crowding it: the first combos, counters and dodges, with the angel blocking and striking beside him. The father, who never sees them, sits frozen. His guardian angel is pinned against the car in chains of shadow. Freed, he tells them the first clue to the larger mystery: the man the father owes, and where the lights first went out.
  5. The choice. He makes his own choice: he drops the gun into a storm drain and drives home. The street's lights come back on. In the last close-up the apartment door opens, and we see her face.
  6. The bell. Back to the chapel as the bell rings for Vigils. The brothers file in. Fr. Lawrence's knees ache.
  • Fr. Lawrence's look. Tall and thin, in a black clerical suit and long black coat, with a black fedora, as priests in the 1940s and 50s wore. His white Roman collar is the brightest thing on him; it always catches the light, so no one can mistake what he is. Tired, kind eyes, perhaps glasses. In the monastery, the habit.
  • Sideways. The game is played with the phone sideways. Where the phone allows, it locks to sideways when play starts; where it does not (iPhones), a clean "turn your phone" card shows until it is turned.
  • Sounds, not voices. No voice acting. Short, restrained sounds: breaths and efforts on the moves, a soft "hah" on a finisher, a sigh in the dialogue. The angel speaks with a faint chord, not a voice. Nothing silly.
  • Saving. Progress is saved after each part.

Build order (after the first build)

  1. Combat arena: the priest and his angel; three demons (one to counter, one to dodge, one with a shield); all the gestures; dizzy and rage; the combo meter; a first Heights/Depths shift.
  2. Michael and tag-ins.
  3. One full encounter: a person, a temptation, the fight, their choice.
  4. Flight prototype: one district in shadow; light-tether, dive and pull-up, updrafts, shadow drain.
  5. The city: districts, reclaiming the light, the virtues, the story.
