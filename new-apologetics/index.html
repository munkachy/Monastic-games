Game design · concept draft

New Apologetics

A turn-based team battler in the style of Star Trek: Legends, where the heroes are today's Catholic apologists and nobody is ever hurt. Opponents are not defeated. When their conviction finally gives way, they flicker, and then they sign up for OCIA.

“Always be ready to make a defense to anyone who asks you for a reason for the hope that is in you; yet do it with gentleness and respect.” 1 Peter 3:15. The four stats of the game come from this verse.

A turn in progress.

The rules

A Legends battle, turned into a debate

Everything keeps the shape of Star Trek: Legends, so its balance carries over: teams of four, turn order by speed, one basic move with no cooldown, two skills on a 3-turn cooldown, and one great skill on a 4-turn cooldown that starts partly charged. Only the meaning changes.

Win the man, not the argument

Legends has two ways to beat an enemy: take all his health or break his morale. This game keeps both, but they are not equal. Break an opponent's Conviction and he converts: full reward, and sometimes he joins your roster. Run his Composure to zero first and he storms off embarrassed: small reward, no conversion. Players learn to lower Conviction with patient, charitable arguments instead of just scoring points. This mirrors how conversion really happens. Newman called it the convergence of many probabilities, a cumulative case, not one knockout blow. Cameron Bertuzzi's own Bayesian approach is the same idea in numbers.

The roster

Each apologist plays like a Legends hero

Each apologist uses a Legends hero's kit: the same targets, cooldowns and percentages, and starting stats scaled from that hero's rank-one numbers. The balance is borrowed, and the personality is his own.

Signature move

Camera Mog

Ethan Muse leans right into the lens, slides his glasses down, and looks over the top of them. The battle pauses for a close-up. It is Worf's Klingon Fury in the kit: a boss-breaking 400%, raised to 600% if the target is already Doubting. If it breaks the target's conviction, Ethan gains a pip of his own.

The opposition

Three storylines, three traditions

Each tradition has its own set of rank-and-file debaters and a boss. The rank and file are invented characters. The bosses are real people, so they appear only with their permission.

Instead of defeat

The OCIA flicker

A converted opponent flickers, fades, and leaves behind a sign for the parish OCIA class. The caption is theologically exact for each tradition, so even the victory screen teaches something.

The story

Eight chapters, each ending in a teaching card

Legends tells its story in eight chapters of six missions each. This game does the same, and each chapter ends by unlocking a teaching card. Every card is graded by its theological weight, so a player learns to tell a dogma from a pious opinion.

Dogma defined, to be believed with divine faith Doctrine taught with authority, not defined Private revelation approved, never binding Opinion open question Argument an apologetic case, not Church teaching

    Open questions

    • Permission from everyone depicted, including the bosses. A boss who converts is the joke of the game, and it is also the part most likely to hurt. James White and Jacob Hansen should see their kits and their OCIA scenes before release. Without their yes, their places go to invented characters.
    • The Islam storyline needs a boss. Until one is chosen and agrees, an invented character, the Speakers' Corner Champion, fills the place.
    • GodLogic is a mere-Christian apologist, not a Catholic one. He fits best as a guest ally in the Islam storyline, where Catholics and Protestants make common cause, and that is a good lesson in itself.
    • One roster slot is open. It uses Riker's kit: a bold damage-dealer with a taunt and a bluff.
    • The likenesses are first drafts. They are built from each man's best-known look and will be redrawn from photographs.
