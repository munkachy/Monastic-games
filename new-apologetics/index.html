New Apologetics

A new era in Catholic apologetics has begun, and nothing like it has come before. Scholars know the ancient world better today than any generation since it passed away: early manuscripts, archaeology and the patient study of the first centuries let us read the Gospels and the Fathers in their own setting. The sciences have shown a universe with a beginning and laws finely set for life, and reported Eucharistic miracles have been examined with laboratory tools no earlier age possessed. Above all, the deposit of faith, given once for all to the apostles, has been unfolding under the Magisterium for two thousand years. Nothing new has been added to it, but the Church understands it more deeply than ever, and with it the goodness of God and the depth of his mercy. No Christian before us has had so much at hand to give a reason for the hope that is in him, speaking the truth in love (Ephesians 4:15).

Still, no argument can give faith. Faith is a gift, and conversion is the work of the Holy Spirit. What apologetics can do is clear the ground: pull up the weeds of misunderstanding and roll away the stones of prejudice, so that the seed falls on good soil. This is a turn-based team battler about the apologists doing that work today. Like any good debate, most end with someone conceding the point. But again and again, an opponent comes home.

“Always be ready to make a defense to anyone who asks you for a reason for the hope that is in you; yet do it with gentleness and respect.” 1 Peter 3:15. The whole game grows out of this verse.

Camera Mog

Ethan Muse tips his head toward the lens, lets his glasses slide down his nose, and looks up at you over the top of them. The battle stops for the close-up. It is the hardest-hitting single move any Defender has: a crushing hit on Composure, harder still if the opponent is already Doubting, and his Zeal drops. The rules

A battle, turned into a debate

Teams of four take turns, fastest first: a strip of faces over the stage shows who goes next. Every hero has a basic move that can be used every turn, two skills that need three turns to recharge, and one great move that needs four and starts partly charged. Every debater has just two things to lose: Composure and Zeal. Moves either hit Composure, knock Zeal down, or do something else: stun, mute, call out, shield.

What you seeWhat it means
ComposureHow long a debater keeps his cool. At zero, a hero is Discouraged and sits out until a friend encourages him back.
ZealEveryone starts at 0. Blue arrows up (to +3) when it rises, red arrows down when it falls. At the bottom, an opponent leaves the debate and a hero is Discouraged.
Backing upWhen someone argues, a teammate with Zeal at 0 or higher may jump in with their basic move. The higher the Zeal, the more often it happens. A fired-up team argues as one.
StarsEach debate remembers the hardest difficulty you have won it on: one star for Gentle, two for Normal, three for Hard, four for Very Hard, five for Crucible.
LevelOnly heroes who win a debate gain experience: a lost debate earns nothing, and the bench gains nothing. When a chapter is too hard, go back and win earlier debates to level up. Each level adds 7% to a hero’s Composure and to the strength of his arguments, and opens new moves. A hero who joins the roster later starts a level or two behind your regular team. Bring him along and he catches up fast: a hero debating beside higher-level teammates learns half as much again for each level behind the strongest, up to double.
💡 Move tipsIn a debate, tap the lightbulb on any move to see what it does, how often it can be used, and when it is worth using. It works on locked and waiting moves too, so you can plan ahead.
SpeedEveryone acts once a round, fastest first, and speed never changes. Heroes who act early tend to carry less Composure; those who carry the most take longer to act. Going first lets you shield the team or finish off an opponent before he answers.
DifficultyFive steps, set on the first screen and changed at any time: Gentle (for the story), Normal, Hard, Very Hard and Crucible (“tested like gold in the fire,” 1 Peter 1:7). Harder steps bring tougher, sharper opponents, more podiums and reinforcements, and a little more experience. The tutorial is always gentle.
ReinforcementsOn Hard and above, fresh opponents wait in reserve. When one goes out, the next walks in to take his place. The round counter shows how many are still waiting.
PodiumsSome debaters start behind something: a podium, a soapbox, a stepladder at Speakers' Corner, a pulpit, a streamer's desk. It changes every battle. It takes the hits meant for the debater until it falls. A few moves go straight past it, a few hit it extra hard, and lowering Zeal gets past it every time.
Sent inA few moves send a teammate in to answer at once with their basic move, out of turn. Best used on a friend who hits hard but acts late.
Deflect · Crit · ResilienceEvery debater has a small chance to deflect part of a hit, to land a critical hit (which also knocks the other's Zeal down), and to shrug off a loss of Zeal. Exposed debaters can't deflect.
Hidden strengthsSome heroes hit harder, some take a hit better, some build stronger shields and podiums for their friends. The game never shows these numbers. You learn each hero's gifts by playing them.
DumbfoundedStunned: loses turns while he thinks it over. Stars circle his head, one for each turn he still has to lose, and he sways on his feet. Some moves only stun some of the time: the stars show when it has really happened.
Called OutMust answer one particular apologist and no one else.
Doubting · FlusteredLoses a little Composure each turn. An opponent is left Doubting his position, and some skills hit him harder for it. An apologist never doubts his faith: the same hard question leaves him Flustered.

Winning the debate is not the same as winning the man

An opponent is out of the debate two ways: empty his Composure and he concedes, or knock his Zeal to the bottom and he leaves. Either way you've won the argument, but winning the argument is not winning the man. Aquinas taught that arguments can clear the way to faith but cannot produce it, and Newman called conversion the convergence of many probabilities. Conversion is the Holy Spirit's work, so the game keeps it out of your hands: each time one of the rank and file goes out of the debate, there is an even chance that he converts instead, and you never know which it will be. Your part is to clear the ground. The bosses are not won over in battle, with one exception you find by playing.

The roster

Seventeen heroes, each with a style of their own

Every hero has a role on the team. Leaders lift everyone around them, Scholars weaken the other side, Defenders draw the fire, Encouragers restore their friends, and Builders put up shields. Each hero's moves come from what that apologist is known for. Their strengths stay out of sight: play them and find out.

Every move, animated

The move theater

Ordinary moves share a few animations, as in most battle games. A hero fires off a volley of words, walks over to hand someone something, raises an aura over the team, or throws a hex across the floor. Each hero fills them with their own words and things. Every great move has its own full-screen close-up: Jimmy Akin's UFO case files, Fr. Mike's calendar racing to Day 365, Bishop Barron's stained glass breaking through the beige, Fr. Pine's Summa article writing itself. Two heroes also share a duo great move: Ethan Muse and Fr. Spitzer's Vindicatory Miracles. Pick a hero, then a move.

The opposition

Five chapters, five groups

Each chapter pits the team against one group, with its own rank-and-file debaters and a boss. The rank and file are invented characters.

Carried into every debate

Five books

Each hero can carry one book into a debate, and the books pass freely from hero to hero on the team screen. Every chapter won adds one to the shelf.

The seed on good soil

When someone comes home

When an opponent converts, he flickers, fades, and leaves behind a sign. What the sign says is theologically exact for where he's coming from, so even that moment teaches something. Not everyone goes to OCIA.

The story

Five mysteries, told in cut scenes

Each chapter sets the team a mystery: a question they can't answer at first. A short scene after every battle moves the story along, one clue at a time, and the answer comes out only in the boss debate. When the team gets stuck, anyone on the roster can walk on and pitch in, even someone who wasn't in the last battle. Scott Hahn has a habit of arriving at exactly that moment. Tap the scene to read on.

Each chapter ends by unlocking teaching cards, graded by their theological weight, so a player learns to tell a dogma from a pious opinion.
