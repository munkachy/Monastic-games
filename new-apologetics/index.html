New Apologetics

A turn-based team battler where the heroes are today's Catholic apologists and nobody is ever hurt. Opponents are not defeated. When their conviction finally gives way, they flicker, and then they sign up for OCIA.

“Always be ready to make a defense to anyone who asks you for a reason for the hope that is in you; yet do it with gentleness and respect.” 1 Peter 3:15. The four stats of the game come from this verse.

Camera Mog

Ethan Muse tips his head toward the lens, lets his glasses slide down his nose, and looks up at you over the top of them. The battle stops for the close-up. The rules

A battle, turned into a debate

Teams of four take turns in order of Readiness. Every hero has a basic move that can be used every turn, two skills that need three turns to recharge, and one great move that needs four and starts partly charged. Nobody is ever hurt: the numbers measure composure and conviction.

Stat or statusWhat it means
ComposureHow long a debater keeps his cool. At zero, a hero is Discouraged and sits out until a friend encourages him back.
ConvictionFour pips. When an opponent loses all four, he is converted: he flickers and goes to OCIA.
RhetoricThe strength of arguments from wit and persuasion.
LearningThe strength of arguments from Scripture, history and philosophy.
CharityGentleness and respect. It softens every blow taken.
Readiness“Always be ready.” Sets the turn order.
DumbfoundedLoses turns while he thinks it over.
Called OutMust answer one particular apologist and no one else.
DoubtingLoses a little Composure each turn. Some skills hit Doubting targets harder.
Cross-ExaminedTakes more from arguments based on Learning.
Fact-CheckRemoves a buff.
ExamenRemoves every debuff.
Citation · Charity ShieldBlocks most of the next argument · absorbs arguments for 3 turns.
Muted · RebuttalCan use only his basic move · answers back when addressed.

Win the man, not the argument

An opponent can be beaten two ways, and they are not equal. Break his Conviction and he converts: full reward, and sometimes he joins your roster. Run his Composure to zero first and he storms off embarrassed: small reward, no conversion. Players learn to lower Conviction with patient, charitable arguments instead of just scoring points. This mirrors how conversion really happens. Newman called it the convergence of many probabilities, a cumulative case, not one knockout blow. Cameron Bertuzzi's own Bayesian approach is the same idea in numbers.

The roster

Seventeen heroes, each with a style of their own

Every hero has a role on the team. Leaders lift everyone around them, Scholars weaken the other side, Defenders draw the fire, Encouragers restore their friends, and Builders put up shields. Their numbers are balanced against one another, and each hero's moves come from what that apologist is known for.

Every move, animated

The move theater

Ordinary moves share a few animations, as in most battle games. A hero fires off a volley of words, walks over to hand someone something, raises an aura over the team, or throws a hex across the floor. Each hero fills them with their own words and things. Every great move has its own full-screen close-up: Jimmy Akin's UFO case files, Fr. Mike's calendar racing to Day 365, Bishop Barron's stained glass breaking through the beige, Fr. Pine's Summa article writing itself. Two heroes also share a duo great move: Ethan Muse and Fr. Spitzer's Vindicatory Miracles. Pick a hero, then a move.

The opposition

Five chapters, five groups

Each chapter pits the team against one group, with its own rank-and-file debaters and a boss. The rank and file are invented characters.

Instead of defeat

The OCIA flicker

A converted opponent flickers, fades, and leaves behind a sign for the parish OCIA class. The caption is theologically exact for each tradition, so even the victory screen teaches something.

The story

Five mysteries, told in cut scenes

Each chapter sets the team a mystery: a question they can't answer at first. Short scenes between battles move the story along. When the team gets stuck, anyone on the roster can walk on and pitch in, even someone who wasn't in the last battle. Scott Hahn has a habit of arriving at exactly that moment. Tap the scene to read on.

Each chapter ends by unlocking teaching cards, graded by their theological weight, so a player learns to tell a dogma from a pious opinion.

Dogma defined, to be believed with divine faith Doctrine taught with authority, not defined Private revelation approved, never binding Opinion open question Argument an apologetic case, not Church teaching

    Work in progress

    • The whole game is still under review. The dialogue, the moves and the balance are drafts. The words given to real people are written to reflect what each has said in public.
    • The Islam storyline needs a boss. Until one is chosen and agrees, an invented character, the Speakers' Corner Champion, fills the place.
    • GodLogic is a mere-Christian apologist, not a Catholic one. He fits best as a guest ally in the Islam storyline, where Catholics and Protestants make common cause, and that is a good lesson in itself.
    • The likenesses are mostly drawn from photographs. A few are still best guesses and will be redrawn as better photographs come in.
