The idea

The game is built like Kingdom Two Crowns. In that game a king rides left and right along one long strip of land, drops coins, and a kingdom grows from a campfire into a castle, while every night creatures come out of the dark to tear it down. Here, the player is not a king. You are the angel of a church, the "angel of the church of Ephesus" to whom the first letter of the Apocalypse is written. The people cannot see you, and you do not command them. You walk among them, carrying their prayers and alms as small lights, and you put those lights where they are needed. Wanderers come in from the woods. A cross in the wilderness becomes a house church, then a chapel, then a stone church the bishop comes to consecrate. Out past the fields, a monastery rises. And every night the seven capital sins come out of the dark, not to kill your people, but to lead them away.

There is one island for each of the seven churches of Asia. Each has its own trouble, taken from its own letter. Ephesus is losing its first love. Smyrna is poor and must endure ten days of tribulation. Sardis is asleep and must learn to keep watch. Laodicea is rich and lukewarm. The game ends at Laodicea, at a door, with someone knocking.

The seven stars are the angels of the seven churches. And the seven candlesticks are the seven churches.

Apocalypse 1:20

The look comes from While You Have the Light: black silhouettes and flame. The sky, though, now changes with the hour, and the water in front reflects everything above it. It is made for a phone held sideways, played with one thumb.

How Kingdom Two Crowns works

This is a summary of the game we are borrowing from, gathered from players' guides (sources at the end). The numbers are the fan guides' figures and are given only as a measure; we will tune our own.

One strip of land, one action

The monarch rides left and right on a horse. The only thing the player can do, besides move, is drop coins from a purse. Every place that can be bought shows a row of empty coin slots above it; you fill the slots and the thing happens. There is almost no text and no tutorial beyond a few first steps: light the campfire, hire two people, buy a bow and a hammer, build a wall on each side. Everything else is learned by watching.

The people mostly ignore the king. You never give orders. You decide only where the money goes, and they do the rest. They come to you only to hand over the coins they have earned.

Building up

The town centre has seven levels, from a campfire to a castle. Each level opens new shops and stronger walls. Walls go up through five levels, from a barricade to iron, and towers give archers a place to stand. Builders clear the forest to make room. This opens the land behind the trees, but cutting too close to a vagrant camp destroys it. Some islands have mines that unlock stone and later iron.

The Greed

Every night creatures called the Greed come out of portals at the edges of the land.

  • Greedlings are small and many. If one touches a villager, the villager loses, in order, his spare coins, his tool, and his last coin. Then he is a vagrant again.
  • Breeders are big; they smash walls and throw stones at towers.
  • Floaters fly in and carry archers off their towers.
  • Crown stealers leap the walls and go for the crown alone.

When the monarch is hit, he drops one coin for each blow. When he has no coins left, the crown falls. If it is carried off, the reign is over.

Blood moons, seasons and portals

About every seventh night comes a blood moon: more and stronger Greed. A quiet night follows it. The year has four seasons of about sixteen days each. In winter the farms stop, the game animals vanish, and you live on what you saved. Late on an island, squires lead squads out to destroy the portals. Each destroyed portal brings a revenge attack, even by day. At the end you send bombs into the Greed's cave and destroy its nest, and the island is free.

Islands, and losing

The campaign has five islands. On each you repair a wrecked boat and sail to the next with the coins in your purse and whoever is aboard. If you lose the crown, you go back to the first island. You keep everything you unlocked, though: destroyed portals stay destroyed, and the islands you left have only fallen into about a hundred days of ruin. Coins in the bank pass to your heir.

The rest

There are five hermits, one per island. Each lives in a hut in the woods; you bring him home, and he turns one building into something new, such as a ballista tower, a bakery or a stable. Statues, paid for with gems, give your archers, farmers or builders a lasting gift. There are mounts with powers of their own, a merchant who brings coins each night, a banker who pays interest, and chests to find. Most of all, there is the feel of it. The game is quiet, with few choices, a steady rhythm of riding and dropping coins, and a world that changes slowly around you as you work. Reviewers keep calling it minimal, and they are right. That bareness is why it holds you.

The angel

Unseen by the people, seen by you. To the player, the angel is a soft white light about the height of a man. Faint streaks of wing come and go, but there is never a full figure. The people do not look at it or turn toward it, just as Kingdom's villagers hardly notice their king. Raphael walked beside Tobias for weeks without being known. The angel is the anchor for the camera and the place the lights fall from.

He cannot be hurt. The sins do not attack the angel. They cannot harm him, and they are not after him. They are after the people. So the angel has no health, no crown to lose, and no danger to himself. Everything at risk belongs to someone else, and that is how it should feel.

At night, he encamps. The sins keep back from the angel. Wherever he stands at night, a small circle of light is drawn around him, and no one inside it can be led away.

The angel of the Lord shall encamp round about them that fear him: and shall deliver them.

Psalm 33:8

The circle is small, and it does not mend walls or stop a wall from breaking. It protects only the few people standing in it. So the night has a choice in it: where will you stand? By the farmers caught outside the wall, by the new people with no roots yet, or at the lampstand? This is the one thing the angel does at night that the king in Kingdom cannot. It is the same choice as in While You Have the Light: in the dark, choosing where your light falls is everything.

He glides. There is no horse and no stamina. The angel moves about as fast as Kingdom's horse at a gallop, a little faster over open ground and a little slower in the deep woods. If speed needs to be earned, a later shrine can grant it.

The lights

Kingdom has coins. We have lights: small flames the angel carries. They are the people's prayers and alms. A farmer brings in his harvest and gives part of it. A watchman prays his night office on the wall. On Sunday the whole parish gathers at Mass. Each gift rises as a small flame, and the angel gathers it up as he passes. Lights pour in on Sunday, and the week runs on what Sunday brought, so the week has a rhythm of its own.

When thou didst pray with tears, and didst bury the dead … I offered thy prayer to the Lord.

Tobias 12:12, the angel Raphael

The angel spends the lights the way the king spends coins. Each place you can build shows empty slots, and you fill them by holding still and letting the lights fall. One light on a wanderer, and he comes in. Three on a marked tree, and a builder will cut it. The point is this: the angel is not buying anything. He is offering what the people gave, for the things the people need. The lights are the church's own prayers and alms, put to work. They are not the angel's wages, and they are not grace for sale. See Theology.

Lights show where they are. The angel's light grows brighter as he carries more, and dims as he spends. A small arc of flames rides above him, never more than a row, so you can always count them at a glance. The purse needs no other picture.

Taking root

The catechist is the one new role, and it comes from the parable of the sower. A new parishioner is seed on rock: he has no roots yet, and the sins can lead him away with very little. Once a catechist has spent a few days with him, he is good ground, and he is much harder to lead away. On rich islands like Laodicea, a third state appears: the thorns. People who are comfortable and busy are no longer easy to lead away, but they slowly stop giving. So the catechist matters more and more as the islands go on.

Names

Kingdom's villagers have no names. Ours get one when they take root: a saint's name, shown for a moment when the angel passes close. This is the white stone of Pergamum: a new name written, which no man knoweth, but he that receiveth it (Apocalypse 2:17). It costs nothing to make, and it changes how a loss feels.

Led away, and brought back

When a sin reaches a parishioner, he does not die. He drops his tool, gets up, and walks off into the dark after it. By morning he is a wanderer again at a fire in the woods. One light will bring him back, as in Kingdom, but he comes back without his tool and without his roots. Once the church has a priest and a confessional, though, everything changes. A man brought back goes to confession first, and he gets back his tool, his roots and his name. St. Thomas teaches that works done in charity, deadened by sin, live again through penance (Summa III q.89 a.5). This is the prodigal son: the father gives him back the robe and the ring.

Callings: what cannot be bought

In Kingdom, a knight is made by buying him gear. If our game did that with a priest, it would be simony, the sin of Simon Magus who tried to buy the power of the Spirit (Acts 8:18–24). The Church still punishes it (canon 1380). A Catholic player would see it at once, and the game would be teaching something false through its main action. So callings work differently, and the difference is part of the game.

  • The first priest is sent. When the chapel is built (level 3), the bishop's letter comes, and a priest walks in from the road a few days later. The parish is part of a diocese and receives its pastor from the bishop (canon 515). He says Mass every morning, and Sunday's lights double. He hears confessions, and he can lead the procession.
  • Later priests are called from your own people. You can build a small seminary. You can drop lights at the shrine of the harvest, which is prayer for vocations: Pray ye therefore the Lord of the harvest, that he send forth labourers into his harvest (Matthew 9:38). That makes a calling more likely. It never makes one certain, and it never sets the day. Now and then, a man who has taken root puts down his tool and walks to the seminary. After a season he is ready, and he waits for the bishop.
  • The bishop comes; he is never recruited. The bishop's visit is an event, not a person you hire. He comes when the church is ready to be consecrated. He ordains the men waiting at the seminary and confirms the people who have taken root. The visit is the great day of an island.
  • Monks enter by the gate. See The monastery.

So the player can pray, prepare and wait, but cannot buy. Every other part of the game moves at the speed of your spending. This one part moves at its own pace, and you will feel the difference. That difference is the point.

The night: the seven

The sins of While You Have the Light come back, drawn smaller to fit Kingdom's scale. They come out of the old altars of the gods at the edges of the land. Each sin plays one of Kingdom's parts, with a turn of its own:

Thou shalt not be afraid of the terror of the night. Of the arrow that flieth in the day, of the business that walketh about in the dark: of invasion, or of the noonday devil.

Psalm 90:5–6

Sloth is the noonday devil of the desert monks (Evagrius, Praktikos 12). Kingdom has nothing to do by day except build. Sloth gives the day a threat of its own, and a quiet, creeping one.

The watchmen fight with what the monk had in the dark in While You Have the Light: lantern light and holy water. Inside a lantern's light a sin shows its colour and can be burned. Beyond it, it is a black shape with two pale eyes. Towers at higher levels hold more watchmen and throw a wider light.

The week and the year

A day

To start with, a day is about three minutes and a night about one and a half. The hours are marked by what the church does:

  • Lauds at dawn. The night ends and the people go out to work. Lights from the night office are waiting at the walls.
  • Sext at noon. Sloth's hour.
  • Vespers at dusk. The bell, once you have one, rings the warning: everyone come in.
  • Compline, and then the dark.

A week

Kingdom has a blood moon about every seventh night, and a quiet night after it. Ours falls on Saturday night, every week. Then comes Sunday: no raid that night, the Mass in the morning, and the week's great gift of lights. The week's rhythm is pressure, then rest, then plenty.

The year

Kingdom's four seasons become the church year. Each season tints the sky in its own liturgical colour at dusk.

The lampstand

The lampstand stands before the church door from the first day, and its flame is the life of the church. In Kingdom you lose when the crown is taken. Here you lose when the lampstand is moved out of its place.

Be mindful therefore from whence thou art fallen: and do penance, and do the first works. Or else I come to thee, and will move thy candlestick out of its place, except thou do penance.

Apocalypse 2:5

Note who moves it in the letter: the Lord does, not the enemy. So in the game, the sins can never take the lampstand. What they can do is empty the church. If at dawn no one is left standing at the lampstand, its flame gutters. That is the warning, and it is the "do penance" of the letter: you have one more day to bring even one person back. If the next dawn finds it still alone, the lampstand is moved. The screen goes quiet, and the island is lost.

What follows is copied from Kingdom. You go back to Ephesus. Everything you unlocked stays unlocked. Altars you overthrew stay overthrown. The islands you left have fallen into some months of ruin, but they have not vanished. After Sardis, the book of life carries over too: the people who had taken root and had a name are waiting for you when you come back (Apocalypse 3:5).

Pride's flight to the lampstand is still dangerous, even so. If it reaches the lamp and no one is standing there, the flame drops low and the whole church loses a night's lights. That is enough to make you guard it.

The wild: what there is to find

Kingdom's pleasure is riding out past the walls to see what is there. Ours has the same. Each island is laid out by hand, with a few things to find in its woods and hills:

  • Wanderers' fires. These are where people come from. Clearing the woods too close to one puts it out for good, as in Kingdom.
  • The island's saint, who plays the part of Kingdom's hermit. He or she waits somewhere in the wild. Bring them in, and they turn one building into something new. See The seven churches.
  • Ruins of an older church, with lights buried in them. These are our chests.
  • Holy wells and wayside shrines. A shrine, once lit, gives one kind of worker a lasting gift.
  • The old altars of the gods, where the sins come from at night. A priest and a band of watchmen can go out and overthrow one, as St. Boniface felled the oak of Thor at Geismar and built a chapel from its wood. The sins answer with a revenge night, as in Kingdom.
  • The seat. At the far end of each island is the sins' own place, the "seat of Satan" of the letter to Pergamum (Apocalypse 2:13). Once the shrine stands and the altars are down, the priest carries the Blessed Sacrament out in procession, with every watchman and a crowd of people behind him. It is a long, slow walk to the dark at the end of the land. Tradition says that when soldiers came over the wall at San Damiano, St. Clare had the Sacrament carried out to meet them, and they fled. The procession frees the island. It is that island's last act, and its finest.
  • The shore. The boat lies here, and the boat leaves from here.

Clearing trees changes the land you can see. The black forest in front thins out, and the far hills, the sky and the light come through. An island you have worked on looks different from one you have just landed on. You can see your work at a glance.

The monastery

From the third island on, a hermit's hut in the far woods can grow into a monastery. It is a second centre of light, standing out on the edge, away from the town, as real foundations did. It works by the two halves of St. Benedict's motto:

  • Labora. The monks farm, brew and copy books, and the monastery brings in lights of its own, steadily, even in Lent.
  • Ora. At night the monks sing the Night Office. While they sing, a wide light stands over that whole side of the land, and the sins will not cross it. The monastery is a wall made of prayer.

Entering. No one is sent to a monastery and no one is bought. A man who has taken root may, now and then, leave his tool and walk out to the monastery gate. Then he waits. The Rule says exactly what happens next:

When anyone newly comes for the reformation of his life, let him not be granted an easy entrance … If the newcomer, therefore, perseveres in his knocking, and if it is seen after four or five days that he bears patiently the harsh treatment offered him and the difficulty of admission, and that he persists in his petition, then let entrance be granted him.

The Rule of St. Benedict 58.1–4

So he sits at the gate for four or five days, whatever the weather and whatever comes out of the dark. The angel can stand by him at night. Then the gate opens and a new monk goes in. On the island of Laodicea, someone else knocks at a door. See below.

The guesthouse. Let all guests who arrive be received like Christ (RB 53.1). With a guesthouse, wanderers come in on their own, a few each week.

The abbot. Once there are twelve monks, they elect an abbot (RB 64). This is an event, like the bishop's visit, and from then on the monastery keeps the hours on its own.

The seven churches

The seven letters (Apocalypse 2–3) follow the road a messenger would take from Patmos: landing at Ephesus, north to Smyrna and Pergamum, then round through Thyatira, Sardis and Philadelphia to Laodicea. The islands come in that order. Each letter gives its island one trouble, one saint, and one gift that you take with you. The only words the game shows are each letter's first line when you land and its promise when you sail.

IEphesus

But I have somewhat against thee, because thou hast left thy first charity.

Apocalypse 2:4
The trouble
The first love grows cold. Anyone the angel has not passed near for several days grows dim, then wanders off to the edge on his own, with no sin needed. The lesson of the first island is the game's main action: keep walking among them all. This is where the game's basics are learned: the cross, the first people, the first walls, the first nights.
The saint
St. John the Apostle. An old tradition places him at Ephesus in his old age, with the Mother of the Lord in his care. He unlocks the Lady shrine: people near it do not grow cold.
The gift
To eat of the tree of life (2:7): orchards. Fruit trees bring in lights all year, after a slow start.

IISmyrna

You shall have tribulation ten days. Be thou faithful until death: and I will give thee the crown of life.

Apocalypse 2:10
The trouble
Poverty, then ten nights. Lights are scarce here, about half of what other islands give. Somewhere in its second year comes the tribulation: ten nights in a row, each as fierce as a Saturday night. Smyrna's letter, like Philadelphia's, has no rebuke in it, so the island has no new way to lose people. You only have to hold out.
The saint
St. Polycarp, bishop of Smyrna, burned in its stadium: eighty-six years have I served him, and he has done me no wrong (Martyrdom of Polycarp 9). He unlocks the martyrs' shrine. The Smyrnaeans gathered his bones as "more precious than precious stones" (ch. 18), one of the first accounts we have of keeping relics and a martyr's day. At the shrine, the people stand firm: no one within its light can be led away.
The gift
Stone. The quarry, and with it the stone church (level 4). This is Kingdom's stone mine.

IIIPergamum

I know where thou dwellest, where the seat of Satan is: and thou holdest fast my name.

Apocalypse 2:13
The trouble
The seat is close. The sins' own place is near the church, not at the far end, so the nights are short and fierce. Then there is the teaching of Balaam: a trader's stall appears outside the wall, offering lights from the feasts of the idols. The light is real, and plentiful. But each one taken makes a sin's altar grow stronger.
The saint
St. Antipas, my faithful witness, who was slain among you (2:13). The only one of the seven whose name is in the letter itself. He unlocks the scriptorium. Pergamum gave its name to parchment, pergamena, and the books are copied on it. They bring in lights, and later they make the catechists faster.
The gift
The hidden manna, and a white counter, and in the counter a new name (2:17): the bakery, which feeds the people through Lent, and names for everyone who takes root.

The monastery can first be founded from here on.

IVThyatira

I know thy works, and thy faith, and thy charity, and thy ministry, and thy patience, and thy last works which are more than the former. But I have against thee a few things: because thou sufferest the woman Jezabel, who calleth herself a prophetess, to teach, and to seduce my servants.

Apocalypse 2:19–20
The trouble
The enemy within. Everything grows faster here: the "last works more than the former." But one figure lives inside your own walls, a false prophetess with her own small fire. People who go near her fire are led away by day, with no sin in sight. You cannot remove her with lights. Only the bishop's visit can. So the island is a race: will the church be ready for him before she has taken too many?
The saint
St. Lydia, a seller of purple, of the city of Thyatira (Acts 16:14), the first convert recorded in Europe, who opened her house to St. Paul. She unlocks the guesthouse for the town, and the trading guilds, Thyatira's own trade: a market where lights can be traded honestly for stone and iron.
The gift
I will give him the morning star (2:28): Lauds comes earlier. From here on, every night is a little shorter.

VSardis

Thou hast the name of being alive: and thou art dead. Be watchful … If then thou shalt not watch, I will come to thee as a thief, and thou shalt not know at what hour I will come to thee.

Apocalypse 3:1–3
The trouble
Asleep. You land at a great church that is already standing, with tall walls and many houses. Almost no one lives in it, and those who do are asleep. There is no dusk on this island and no warning. The sins come at any hour of the night, without a sign. Sardis's citadel stood on a cliff thought impossible to climb, and it was taken by surprise in the night twice: by Cyrus (Herodotus 1.84) and by Antiochus III (Polybius 7.15–18). You must build watchmen and a bell, so that the town has warning again.
The saint
St. Melito, bishop of Sardis, who wrote one of the oldest Easter homilies we have, On the Pascha. He unlocks the paschal candle, which burns at the church all through Eastertide and lights the whole town each night.
The gift
I will not blot out his name out of the book of life (3:5): the book of life. From now on, if a lampstand is moved, the people with names come back with you.

VIPhiladelphia

Behold, I have given before thee a door opened, which no man can shut: because thou hast a little strength, and hast kept my word.

Apocalypse 3:8
The trouble
A little strength. This is the smallest island, and lights are few. The ground shakes, too. Philadelphia was ruined by the great earthquake of AD 17, and for years the tremors went on, so that many of its people lived out in the fields (Strabo 12.8.18). Here a quake can crack any wall without warning. But the church door cannot be broken, and wanderers come in steadily on their own. Like Smyrna's, its letter has no rebuke.
The saint
St. Ignatius of Antioch, who passed through on the road to his martyrdom and wrote a letter back to the Philadelphians: Take care to keep one Eucharist; for there is one flesh of our Lord Jesus Christ … one altar, as there is one bishop (To the Philadelphians 4). He is not their bishop, and he does not stay. He is a bishop passing through, and his visit is the island's great event.
The gift
I will make him a pillar in the temple of my God, and he shall go out no more (3:12): iron, and the pillars. Iron walls, and towers that tremors cannot crack. This is Kingdom's iron mine.

VIILaodicea

I know thy works, that thou art neither cold, nor hot … Because thou sayest: I am rich, and made wealthy, and have need of nothing: and knowest not, that thou art wretched, and miserable, and poor, and blind, and naked.

Apocalypse 3:15–17
The trouble
Lukewarm. The richest island: lights flow in, and there are people everywhere. When an earthquake flattened Laodicea in AD 60, it rebuilt itself from its own wealth and asked Rome for nothing (Tacitus, Annals 14.27). Here, though, saved lights tarnish: the angel's spare row slowly dims if he carries lights and does not spend them. The people slide from good ground into the thorns. Sloth comes often, and the other sins rarely. The danger is not that the church falls, but that it goes on standing and is dead inside. A common modern reading points to the town's water. It was piped in from far off and arrived lukewarm, unlike the hot springs at Hierapolis or the cold water of Colossae. That fits the game well, as one reading among others.
The saint
Epaphras, who hath much labour for you, and for them that are at Laodicea (Colossians 4:13). He unlocks the poor box, the one place where lights do not tarnish, because they are given away at once.
The end
When the shrine stands and the procession has freed the island, the night does not come. Instead, at the church door, someone knocks.

Behold, I stand at the gate, and knock. If any man shall hear my voice, and open to me the door, I will come in to him, and will sup with him, and he with me.

Apocalypse 3:20

The angel can go to the door, and every person on the island turns, for the first time, toward the same place. There is no fight at the end. There is a supper. Then the seven lampstands are shown together, lit, and the stars in a right hand above them.

The look

The base is While You Have the Light's: every building, tree, person and hill is a black silhouette, and the only bright things are flame and the sins. Three things are added for this game:

  • The sky changes with the hour. Pale gold at dawn, pale grey-blue at noon, deep orange at dusk, near black at night with the lamps and windows glowing. The land stays black against it, like trees against a sunset. At dusk the season's liturgical colour comes into the sky, violet in Advent and Lent and green in the long summer. The colour lives in the sky only. The land never takes it.
  • Water in front reflects everything. A band of water along the bottom of the screen, as in Kingdom. The way to draw it is cheap. Take the strip of the finished picture just above the water line, flip it upside down, darken it, and shift each thin row a little sideways with a slow wave. Then lay a few bright ripples on top where a lamp or the sun is. Black silhouettes over a coloured, rippling sky will look striking. The angel's light is reflected too, so you can follow it in the water.
  • Layers that open up. Four or five layers of land at different depths, moving at different speeds as the angel moves. The nearest is the forest, black and thick. Clearing it opens the view to the far hills, and a worked island looks wider and brighter than a new one.

The people are small, simple versions of the monk from While You Have the Light, about thirty pixels high, so a whole crowd can be drawn on a phone. They get a warm rim of light on their outlines whenever they stand near a flame, as the monk does. Priests wear a chasuble at Mass and a stole in the confessional. Monks wear the cowl.

Controls

The phone is held sideways, and one thumb is enough.

ToDo this
Glide left or rightHold anywhere on that side of the angel
Go fasterHold further out toward the edge
Drop a lightHold on the angel himself; lights fall one by one into the slots below, as long as you hold
See a nameGlide close to a person who has taken root; the name comes and goes

Nothing else. Keyboard: arrows or A and D to glide, Shift to go faster, Down or S to drop.

Why so bare: Kingdom's calm comes from having almost nothing to decide moment to moment and one big thing to decide over the week. The clear goal, the small constant rewards (a light chimes into a slot, a wall goes up) and a task that fills the attention without straining it are the conditions for flow. Anything we add to the controls takes away from that.

Theology

Each claim the game leans on is marked with how much weight it carries: Dogma defined or plainly of faith; Taught the Church's ordinary teaching or law; Tradition an old pious tradition, not a matter of faith; Opinion a theological opinion, freely held; Scholars a historical or exegetical reading, open to revision.

Angels, and the angel of a church

Dogma Angels exist. They are pure spirits, made by God (Fourth Lateran Council; Catechism 328–330).

Taught Each person is watched over by an angel: Beside each believer stands an angel as protector and shepherd leading him to life (St. Basil, quoted in Catechism 336). That each of the faithful has a guardian angel of his own is commonly held as certain, though it has not been solemnly defined.

Opinion That communities, churches and nations have angels set over them is an old and widely held opinion. It is drawn from Daniel 10, where the "prince of the kingdom of the Persians" is an angel, and from the angels of the seven churches. Origen held it, and St. Thomas, following Pseudo-Dionysius, gives the care of peoples to the order of Principalities (Summa I q.108 a.6). But it is not defined. Many readers, ancient and modern, take the "angel" of each church in the Apocalypse to be its bishop, since angelos means "messenger". The game takes the first reading as its picture. It does not deny the second, and the bishop's own place in the game keeps that reading present.

Taught Angels serve; they do not rule. Are they not all ministering spirits, sent to minister for them, who shall receive the inheritance of salvation? (Hebrews 1:14). This is why the angel commands no one, and why the people move freely. In the game, as in life, a person invited can still choose.

Lights are prayers and alms, not grace for sale

Taught Scripture shows angels carrying the prayers of the faithful before God: Raphael to Tobias (Tobias 12:12), the angel with the golden censer (Apocalypse 8:3–4), the angel to Cornelius: Thy prayers and thy alms are ascended for a memorial in the sight of God (Acts 10:4). The lights are exactly these: prayers and alms. Spending them on a wanderer is a picture of intercession, the Church praying for those outside her, not of buying a soul. Grace is never earned and never sold. The first movement toward God is always His (Catechism 2001, 2010). The game takes care never to suggest that lights make anyone holy.

Simony, and callings

Taught To buy or sell spiritual things, above all holy orders, is simony, and it is gravely wrong (Acts 8:18–24; Catechism 2121; canon 1380). That is why the game never sells a priest, a monk or a bishop. A pastor is given to a parish by the bishop (canon 515). A calling comes from God, and the Church may pray for it (Matthew 9:38). The shrine of the harvest makes a calling more likely. It works the way prayer works, not the way a purchase works: nothing is owed, and no day is promised. If even that feels too close to a trade, the shrine can be dropped and callings left to chance and time alone.

The sins, and being led away

Taught The seven capital sins are a tradition of spiritual theology (Catechism 1866), not a closed list; see While You Have the Light's notes. In the game the sins lead people away and never kill them. That is truer to temptation, which works by drawing rather than by force (James 1:14), and it keeps the stakes on the soul, not on the body.

Taught Penance restores what mortal sin had cut off. St. Thomas teaches that works done in charity, deadened by sin, revive through penance (Summa III q.89 a.5). That is why confession gives back the tool, the roots and the name.

The lampstand

Scholars The lampstand moved "out of its place" (Apocalypse 2:5) is read by most as the loss of the church's standing and light, not of the city. In the game it is the Lord who moves it, never the enemy: the sins empty the church; they do not snuff out its light by force. The warning dawn before the end is the "do penance" of the same verse.

The Sacrament in procession

Tradition St. Clare meeting the soldiers at San Damiano with the Blessed Sacrament comes from her early Legend and the canonisation process, and is honoured in her images. The game's procession is a public act of faith and worship, like the processions of Corpus Christi. It must never look like the Sacrament being used as a weapon. The sins flee from it; it does not fire at them.

The cities

Scholars What the island notes say about the cities themselves comes from ancient historians and modern readers. The night captures of Sardis are in Herodotus and Polybius. Philadelphia's tremors are in Strabo. Laodicea's refusal of Roman aid is in Tacitus. The lukewarm water is a common modern reading that some now question. Which altar was the "seat of Satan" at Pergamum is debated: the great altar of Zeus, the temple of Asclepius, and the cult of the emperor all have their defenders. The game uses these as colour, never as claims.

Tradition St. John at Ephesus is attested from the second century (St. Irenaeus). That the Mother of the Lord lived there with him is a later tradition; the Church has honoured the house near Ephesus as a place of pilgrimage, without defining it.

Places of worship

Scholars The seven levels loosely follow history: worship in houses (Romans 16:5; the house church at Dura-Europos, about 240), then purpose-built churches, then basilicas after 313. Parishes in the later sense, with their own pastor under a bishop, came after the time of the seven letters. Bells and Benedictine monasteries came later still. The game lets the island grow through the ages on purpose. It is the church of Ephesus across all its centuries, not Ephesus in AD 95.

Still to decide

The name. The Seven Lampstands is a working title. Others: To the Angel of the Church; Lampstand; Walk Among Them.

Can a wanderer refuse? In Kingdom, one coin always works. Should a few wanderers sometimes take the light and still not come, until a second visit, or the catechist, or a feast day? It would say something true about freedom. It would also be the first thing in the game that does not answer at once.

The shrine of the harvest. Keep it, as a picture of praying for vocations? Or drop it, so that nothing the player does touches a calling at all?

Small sins? Kingdom's raids are many small greedlings and a few big ones. Our seven are all big. Do we draw Avarice small and in crowds, or add a lesser shade, such as the "thoughts" of the desert monks, as the swarm?

The monastery: on every island from Pergamum on, or only on some? And does it carry over between islands, as one monastery that founds daughter houses?

How long is an island? Kingdom's islands take a few hours each. On a phone, played a little at a time, is an island one year of game time (about five hours)? Or less?

The end at Laodicea. Is the supper shown, or only the open door and the light coming out of it?
