The idea

A hooded Benedictine monk, in the cowl he wears in choir, carries a torch through a dark world of sharp crags, cliffs, tunnels and floating rock. The torch throws a circle of light. Inside the light the demons show what they are, each sin in its own colour, and he fights them hand to hand. Outside it they are black shapes with two pale eyes. There he cannot fight them hand to hand; he can only throw holy water at them, or whatever he is holding.

It is an action game in the spirit of Castlevania, with fights that flow from one blow to the next. Every blow that lands keeps the flow going, and the flow makes the light grow. So the way to see is to keep going. That is what the sentence from the Prologue of the Rule, which gives the game its name, says. St. Benedict took it from the Lord's own words:

Yet a little while, the light is among you. Walk whilst you have the light, that the darkness overtake you not. And he that walketh in darkness, knoweth not whither he goeth.

John 12:35

The music is built on the old Compline hymn Christe, qui lux es et dies, noctis tenebras detegis: "Christ, you are light and day; you uncover the darkness of night." Detegis, "you uncover", is the game in one word.

It is made for a phone held sideways and played with one or two fingers. It can also be played with a keyboard and mouse. The first build is one arena, fought in waves, with a practice beside it.

The look

  • Limbo. As in Limbo (Playdead, 2010), the near world and every figure are black silhouettes. Behind them the mist grows paler with distance, layer on layer of angular mountains. So every black shape stands out against something lighter.
  • Another World. The shapes are flat and sharp, as in Éric Chahi's Another World (1991). The rock is flat on top where he stands, and broken and sharp everywhere else, with spikes hanging beneath every overhang.
  • Only three kinds of colour. The torch flame, the seven sins, and the faint pale blue-white of holy water. Everything else is grey mist and black. A few things show in the dark anyway: the demons' eyes, flames, holy water and sparks.

  • The Ladder of Divine Ascent. The icon at St. Catherine's Monastery on Sinai (12th century) shows monks climbing the thirty rungs of St. John Climacus's ladder toward Christ, while small black demons try to pull them off. That is the picture the game sets in motion: a monk climbing, and small black shapes in the air around him.
  • Prince of Persia. The movement follows Jordan Mechner's Prince of Persia (1989), which was drawn from film of a real person moving. It has weight: a few steps to get running, a crouch before each jump, the hang from a ledge and the pull-up with the legs kicking, the careful step, the turn on the spot.
  • The monk. A black habit with the cowl over it: a long robe to the ankles, very wide sleeves, the deep hood up with its point falling behind. His face is lost in the hood. A thin warm rim of torchlight runs along his edges, so he can always be seen. He holds the torch in his far hand. The near hand is free for blows, for catching, and for carrying.
  • The demons. Each has its own outline, so it can be told apart even as a black shape. In the light it is drawn in its own colours, with flat, angular shading. They are inhuman, never gory, and Lust is never shown immodestly.

The light

The torch casts a circle of light around its flame. Rock blocks the light and casts shadows, so a demon behind a crag stays dark even when it is near.

In the lightIn the dark
The demonsShown in their true colours. You can see what each one is.Black shapes with two pale eyes.
What he can doFly at them and fight hand to hand.Throw holy water, or throw what he is holding.
Things thrown at himSlow down and glint: catch them, or send them back.Come at full speed.
How he movesThe quick speed.The careful dark speed.
  • The light grows with the flow. Each link in a fight widens it. Its reach grows from about twice the monk's height to nearly five times. When the flow ebbs, the light shrinks back. In the flare it floods out far wider still.
  • The torch is also a weapon. The light sits on the flame, so when he swings the torch in a blow, the circle of light swings with it.
  • The flame gutters. When a demon strikes him, the flame sputters, and the light shrinks and flickers for a moment.

The colours are a picture of discernment. In the dark you can tell only that something is there. In the light you see what it is, and so you know how to fight it. The desert fathers prized this gift above the others (Cassian, Conferences 2).

But all things that are reproved, are made manifest by the light; for all that is made manifest is light.

Ephesians 5:13

And the light shineth in darkness, and the darkness did not comprehend it.

John 1:5

He moves in two ways, and the light decides which.

In the dark he goes as a man goes at night with a lamp: one careful step at a time. When the enemy comes into the light, the same light makes him quick. In the quick speed the view also draws back a little, so you can see more of the fight.

Thy word is a lamp to my feet, and a light to my paths.

Psalm 118:105

Point and go

You never steer him. You point where he should go, and he finds the way himself: walking, stepping up and down, climbing, leaping across gaps and dropping from ledges. While a route is chosen, it shows for a moment as a faint trail of embers. A point he cannot reach takes him as close as he can get. If he is in the middle of something he cannot break off, such as a climb, or a blow before it lands, your next order waits for him.

There are no bottomless pits. Every drop lands somewhere. A fall costs time, never a life.

Things to throw, catch and send back

  • Lying about. Stones, jars, beams, skulls and burning brands lie about the Hollow, and three flasks of holy water. Tap one and he goes and picks it up. A flask goes into his store. Anything else he carries in his free hand; then tap any demon, in the light or the dark, and he throws it.
  • What they do. A beam knocks a demon flat. A jar shatters. Anything that hits a broken demon casts it out. Every hit counts in the flow.
  • The demons throw too. Avarice gathers things into its sack and throws them from the dark. Gluttony spits back what it has swallowed. Pride casts violet shards. Lust, Envy and Wrath may now and then pick up what lies at their feet and throw it.
  • Catch. Inside the light, a thing thrown at him slows to a third of its speed and glints. Tap it, and he snatches it out of the air. That is a link in the flow, and now he holds it, ready to throw.
  • Send it back. Swipe on it instead, and he bats it back. It flies to the demon that threw it, if that one lies the way you swiped, or else to the nearest demon that way. It strikes twice as hard, counts double in the flow, and lights the demon up in its colour for a moment, even in the dark. Pride's shards cannot be caught; a tap sends them back too.
  • Parry. A block at the last instant sends a thrown thing straight back at the one who threw it.
  • Gluttony swallows anything thrown at it, his throws too, and spits it back.

Holy water

  • Thrown into the dark. Tap a demon in the dark and he lobs a flask at it in an arc, aimed at where it will be. The flask bursts. The splash burns every demon near it and lights them up in their colours for a moment. Holy water burns Gluttony double. A broken demon caught in the splash is cast out.
  • Only so many. He starts with five flasks and can carry nine. Flasks come back in these ways: one after every wave; one for each flask found lying about; the flasks that spill from Avarice's hoard; and two from the finisher Scattered. Avarice's touch steals one. With none left, there is a dry click, and he goes toward the demon instead.
  • Seen in the dark. A flask always glows a faint pale blue-white. Holy things can be seen in the dark.

Tapping a demon to throw holy water comes from Canticle Mansion. For what holy water is, and what it is not, see Theology, kept straight.

Health, flow and oil

Three things are kept apart. Each has its own mark at the top of the screen (the lives and the oil in the left corner, the flow in the middle), and each works in its own way.

The flow

A link is anything that goes right: a blow landing, a catch, a returned throw hitting, a parry, a finisher, holy water hitting. A blow counts once for each demon it strikes (up to three); a thing sent back, a parry and most finishers count twice; Cast Out counts three times. Each link keeps the flow going for three seconds. If no new link comes in time, the flow does not vanish at once. It ebbs away a link at a time. The light grows with the flow, the music builds with it, and the oil fills with it. At 5, 12 and 20 links the count is called out.

Health

Six lives. A blow from a demon costs one. It also halves the flow and makes the flame gutter. Gluttony's blow and Wrath's charge throw him down. For a second after a blow, nothing can touch him.

Two of the seven strike differently. Avarice's touch steals a flask of holy water instead of a life, as long as he has one. Sloth's touch takes no life at all, but it drains the flow to nothing and half the oil.

When the last life goes, the light goes out. The screen says so, with John 12:35, and shows the wave reached, the demons cast out, the longest flow, and the best wave so far.

Why light is not health

It would be simple to make the light his health: the more he is hurt, the less he sees. That was decided against, for three reasons.

  • A player in trouble would see less just when he most needs to see. One mistake would bring on the next, and the fight would slide downhill.
  • The light is the fruit of watchfulness, not a store of strength. It grows while he keeps going and shrinks when he stops. A wound can make it gutter for a moment, but cannot put it out.
  • It is truer. A soul can be wounded and still see clearly. And a soul can be quite unhurt and yet grow dim through slackness. That is acedia, and that is what Sloth does.

The oil

The oil is the reserve for the flare. It cannot be borrowed at the last moment; it is gathered beforehand, link by link. So it was for the ten virgins (Matthew 25:1–13):

But the wise took oil in their vessels with the lamps.

Matthew 25:4

And the foolish said to the wise: Give us of your oil, for our lamps are gone out.

Matthew 25:8

The flare

When the vial holds enough oil (almost a third of the flask, up to the mark on the vial), spread two fingers apart, and the torch flares.

  • The light swells huge, and every demon in it is shown.
  • Time slows almost to a stop. The world moves at about an eighth of its speed; the monk at half.
  • The music is muffled, as if heard through a wall. A slow heartbeat sounds close by, and a low choir holds one chord. The sounds of the fight stay clear.
  • The oil burns fast while it lasts: about a fifth of the flask each second.

One stroke, one flow. While the flare lasts, draw one stroke through the demons you want, in order, up to eight. Let go, and he goes through them one after another, a different blow for each, as one unbroken flow. These blows strike hard, and Envy cannot turn them aside. A broken demon on the stroke is cast out on the way. If the oil runs out while you are drawing, he goes through the ones already marked. A tap during the flare acts as usual, in slow time.

The seven

Each sin fights in its own way, so its colour, once the light shows it, tells you how to fight it. They come up out of cracks in the rock. As in the Batman Arkham games, only a few strike at a time. They take turns, and the rest prowl in the dark at a distance.

Sloth, the noonday devil

The old name for this sin is acedia. Evagrius calls its demon "the noonday demon" and says it troubles the monk more than any other (Praktikos 12). It comes in the middle of the day, makes the day seem endless, and makes the monk hate his cell and his work and long to be somewhere else. Evagrius took the name from the psalm, which in the Greek and the Latin speaks of a demon at noon:

Of the arrow that flieth in the day, of the business that walketh about in the dark: of invasion, or of the noonday devil.

Psalm 90:6

St. Benedict knows it too. He sends seniors round the monastery in the reading time to see that no brother is acediosus, wasting the time in idleness or chatter (RB 48:18). So Sloth in the game never wounds. It does not knock him down; it empties him. It takes away the zeal and the reserve, and leaves him standing in a small light.

Finishers

As in Mortal Kombat, a beaten foe is not simply gone. A demon brought to nothing is broken: kneeling, swaying, its colour showing through cracks in it. It stays so for about three and a half seconds. Finish it with a gesture on it; each finisher gives something different. If it is not finished in time, it rises again with about a third of its strength.

The virtues

Each sin is finished best by its contrary: Humility against Pride, Generosity against Avarice, Chastity against Lust, Kindness against Envy, Temperance against Gluttony, Patience against Wrath, Diligence against Sloth. He does not strike; he kneels. The light floods, and every other demon of that sin on the field falls broken. That is the point of the picture: a vice is not overcome only by fighting it blow by blow, but by the opposite good taking root. The old monastic writers give each vice its own remedy. The tidy list of seven pairs is a later teaching aid; see Theology, kept straight.

The music

The melody is the hymn Christe, qui lux es et dies, an old Compline hymn. Many medieval uses sang it in Lent; the Cistercian hymnary gives it for Compline on Sundays. The game uses the tune as the Cistercians sang it (they too keep the Rule), from GregoBase no. 9743: the Hymnarium cisterciense, Karlsruhe, Badische Landesbibliothek, Lichtenthal 28, about 1250. It is in the second mode, with its home on D, and moves in a narrow, low range. The harmony keeps to the mode and never uses the sharp seventh of a major key.

Christe, qui lux es et dies,
noctis tenebras detegis,
lucisque lumen crederis,
lumen beatis praedicans.

Christ, you are light and day; you uncover the darkness of night. We believe you are the light of light, and you proclaim light to the blessed.

The arena song

The fight's song moves at 88 beats a minute and is built in layers. The game adds and takes away layers with the flow. A new layer comes in on the next beat; a layer falls away at the next bar, so the changes always land in time. When the light first finds a demon there is a low boom on the beat; when the flow breaks, a falling breath.

LevelWhenWhat plays
0No demon in the light.The mountain wind, a drone on D and A, a bell far off, and two lines of the hymn, high and far off, on a soft flute-like organ stop.
1Demons in the light; flow 0 to 4.A heartbeat on the frame drum, a soft pulse, and an organ chord swelling each bar. The far-off flute now plays every line.
2Flow 5 to 11.A slow pattern on the frame drum, tom-toms at the end of each line, and a harpsichord in broken chords.
3Flow 12 to 19.The great drum, the organ playing the hymn with a second voice below it, mostly a fifth down (as the oldest written harmony did), but always on a note of the chord, and a low figure on the bass, played over and over.
4Flow 20 and more.A men's choir and the full organ, with everything in quicker notes.
  • Under the flare the music is muffled: in a quarter of a second it closes in until only the low notes come through (a filter at about 550 Hz). A slow heartbeat sounds close by, and a low choir holds one chord. The sounds of the fight are not muffled; they stay crisp in the slow time. When the flare ends, the music opens again quickly with a rising rush.
  • The title has the hymn alone on the organ, slowly: first on a soft flute-like stop over a drone, then in fifths, with a bell at the start of each verse.
  • The sounds are short and restrained, made for small phone speakers: the grab of a ledge, the roar of the torch swing, the bright ring of a parry, the glassy hiss of holy water, the sinking tone of Sloth's touch.

The arena (this first build), and practice

The Hollow

A deep cleft in the mountain, much wider and taller than the screen: thirty-two times the monk's height from side to side, and sixteen times from top to bottom. Terraced cliffs rise on both sides. On the left a tunnel runs into a cave. A spire of rock stands in the middle. A ladder of floating crags, each one tapering to a spike beneath, rises to the top of the world. There are no bottomless pits. The demons come up out of eight cracks in the rock, from those far from him and out of the light where they can. Fourteen things lie about to be picked up and thrown, and three flasks of holy water.

Practice

From the title screen. Nothing can hurt you, holy water never runs out, and every finisher is open. You choose which of the seven come, and how many are on the field at once, from one to six. You can also turn on endless oil, to flare as often as you like. In practice the demons do not grow harder, and two at most strike at a time. Demons cast out in practice do not count toward opening the virtues: since every finisher is open there anyway, only the arena counts.

Around the fight

  • The pause lists every move. From it you can go on, begin again, switch between the arena and practice, turn the sound off, or go back to the title. The game pauses by itself when the phone is put aside.
  • Hints. The first time something new happens (the first demon in the light, the first thing thrown at him, the first broken demon, the oil ready), one plain line explains it, once.
  • Sideways. Where the phone allows, the game turns sideways when play starts. Where it does not, a card asks you to turn the phone.

The first level (later)

Not in this build. The arena is where the fighting is learned; the level is the climb it is for.

  • The mountain. A climb up the mountain at night: first down through the tunnels, then up cliff faces and floating ledges toward the summit.
  • The lamps. Unlit lamps line the way. Light one with the torch and it becomes a pool of light, and a place to begin again when the light goes out. It recalls the ancient lighting of the lamps at evening, with the hymn O gladsome light (Phos hilaron).
  • The cloud. At the summit, the cloud. There the torch is set down.

And the people stood afar off. But Moses went to the dark cloud wherein God was.

Exodus 20:21

St. Gregory of Nyssa (Life of Moses, book II) and Dionysius (Mystical Theology 1.3) read this darkness not as the darkness of sin, but as the place where God is beyond all knowing: a light too bright to see. So the game ends where its own darkness turns inside out. All the way up, darkness is the enemy's ground. At the top, the darkness is God's, and the little light he carried is no longer needed.

Theology, kept straight

The demons

  • Defined. The devil and the other demons were created good by God, but became evil by their own doing (Fourth Lateran Council, 1215; Catechism 391). They are fallen angels.
  • Real, but limited. Their power is real, but it is the power of a creature. "He is only a creature, powerful from the fact that he is pure spirit, but still a creature. He cannot prevent the building up of God's reign" (Catechism 395).

    And God is faithful, who will not suffer you to be tempted above that which you are able: but will make also with temptation issue, that you may be able to bear it.

    1 Corinthians 10:13
  • Common teaching. A demon cannot move the will. It can work only on the senses and the imagination, and suggest (St. Thomas, ST I q.111 a.2–4; q.114; I-II q.80 a.1–3). Not every temptation comes from a demon; many come from our own flesh and from the world (ST I q.114 a.3). But every man is tempted by his own concupiscence, being drawn away and allured (James 1:14).
  • A picture. One demon for each sin is a way of speaking, not a claim that each sin has a demon assigned to it. It is the desert's way of speaking: Evagrius writes of "the demon of acedia" (Praktikos 12), and speaks of the other thoughts in the same way. The vice itself is in us; the demon can only press on it.
  • The fight is a picture too. Blows and kicks stand for the spiritual combat, as in the Ladder icon. The monk is not performing a solemn exorcism, which only a priest may do, and only with the bishop's express leave (Code of Canon Law, can. 1172).

    For our wrestling is not against flesh and blood; but against principalities and powers, against the rulers of the world of this darkness, against the spirits of wickedness in the high places.

    Ephesians 6:12
  • Cast out, not killed. A spirit does not die: angels are by nature immortal (ST I q.50 a.5). A demon finished in the game goes up in ash and leaves the field: it is driven off, not ended.

The seven capital sins

The seven are a tradition of spiritual theology, not a defined list. Evagrius (d. 399) wrote of eight "thoughts": gluttony, fornication, avarice, sadness, anger, acedia, vainglory and pride (Praktikos 6). Cassian brought them to the West (Institutes V–XII; Conferences 5). St. Gregory the Great made pride the root of all the rest and named seven that spring from it: vainglory, envy, anger, sadness, avarice, gluttony and lust (Moralia in Job XXXI.45). Later writers folded vainglory into pride and set acedia back in the place of sadness. That gives the list the game uses. The Catechism names them as the capital sins "which Christian experience has distinguished, following St. John Cassian and St. Gregory the Great" (Catechism 1866). So the list is old, well tried and very useful; but it is a way of ordering the vices, not a dogma, and the number is not a fence.

The seven contrary virtues (Humility, Generosity, Chastity, Kindness, Temperance, Patience, Diligence) are a later teaching aid. The principle behind them is old: the monastic writers heal each vice with the good opposite it. For acedia, the remedy in the desert is to stay and persevere; St. Thomas sets acedia, a "sadness about spiritual good", against the joy that comes from charity (ST II-II q.35). The Catechism says it "goes so far as to refuse the joy that comes from God" (Catechism 2094). "Diligence" is the popular name; see Still to decide.

Holy water

Holy water is a sacramental, not a sacrament (Catechism 1667–1670). It does not work by its own force, nor automatically. It works by the prayer of the Church, and it helps the one who uses it with faith: "Sacramentals do not confer the grace of the Holy Spirit in the way that the sacraments do, but by the Church's prayer, they prepare us to receive grace and dispose us to cooperate with it" (Catechism 1670). The older Roman Ritual blesses the water so that it may put to flight every power of the enemy. In the game a flask always burns the demon it reaches. That is a picture of the Church's prayer at work, not a claim that holy water is a weapon that works by itself.

Two darknesses

The game's darkness is the darkness of sin and death: the darkness of John 12:35 and of the Prologue. It is not the dark cloud of Exodus 20:21. That second darkness, the darkness of God beyond knowing, comes only at the very end (see The first level), and the two must never be confused.

The light is given

The flow is a picture of watchfulness and zeal, not of grace earned by effort. The light is God's gift: For thou lightest my lamp, O Lord (Psalm 17:29). The monk's part is to keep it burning, as the wise virgins kept oil for their lamps. And the colours are a picture of discernment: the light does not make the demons evil; it shows what they already are (Ephesians 5:13).

Still to decide

Hooded monks in the dark? Should the demons in the dark look like hooded monks, like him, until the light shows them? Then the light would matter even more: in the dark you could not tell a brother from an enemy.

And no wonder: for Satan himself transformeth himself into an angel of light.

2 Corinthians 11:14

It must be done with care, so that real monks never look sinister: perhaps only the outline at a distance, never close.

How the level's story begins. Who is he, and why does he climb the mountain at night? What sends him out, and what is at the top for him? The arena needs no story; the level does.

Does Sloth need anything more? It never wounds; it drains the flow and the oil, and a block turns it aside. Is that enough of a threat to make it worth hunting? Or should it be harder to keep track of, slipping through the rock out of sight?

The virtue against Sloth. "Diligence" is the name in the popular list. Joy, or Perseverance, would be closer to Evagrius and St. Thomas. Which should the game use?
